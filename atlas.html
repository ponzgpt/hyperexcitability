Muscle hyperexcitability & cramps

Every phenomenon in this field can be placed on three axes: where it starts, what the EMG hears, and why it happens. Learn the axes and the individual diseases fall into place.

Dr Federica Montagnese · Friedrich-Baur-Institut, LMU Munich Watch the talk (62 min) ↗
nerve neurogenic, presynaptic muscle myogenic, postsynaptic bg background added, not stated in the talk 12:20 jumps to that moment of the video
The thesis in five lines
  1. Two compartments.Hyperexcitability starts either in the motor nerve or in the muscle fibre. Each side has its own repertoire of signs.
  2. The EMG is the first fork.On the muscle side, only myotonia produces discharges. Rippling, Brody stiffness and contractures are electrically silent.
  3. Same sign, different meaning.Cramps and fasciculations are usually benign. A few features mark the ones that signal disease.
  4. History, an extended exam and EMG usually make the diagnosis. The routine exam needs additions: test for myotonia, rippling, the warm-up phenomenon and fasciculations.
  5. Most of these conditions can be treated, and an accurate label lets you reassure the patient or give a real prognosis.
Part I

The ontology

Two axes to hang everything on.

Axis 1 · Where

One motor unit, nine places to misfire

  • Presynaptic: motor neuron soma, axon, distal terminals. The source of cramps, fasciculations, myokymia, neuromyotonia and tetany.
  • Synaptic: the neuromuscular junction. Rarely the culprit; Schwartz–Jampel is the example.
  • Postsynaptic: membrane channels, caveolae, calcium release and reuptake, energy supply. The source of myotonia, rippling, Brody stiffness and contractures.

Part II

The machinery

Excitation–contraction coupling, read as a list of failure points. 2:50

  • Skeletal muscle has three properties: excitability, contractility, extensibility.
  • Hyperexcitability syndromes come from disorders of the first two.
  • Four levels can fail: ion channels, the DHPR–RyR1 coupling, ATP supply, and Ca²⁺ reuptake.
Reference

The EMG ruler: firing rate tells discharges apart

  • Several discharges look alike on screen. Rate and rhythm separate them.
  • Myotonic and neuromyotonic discharges can sound similar. The neuromyotonic one fires much faster.
  • The ranges are approximate textbook valuesbg. The talk gives the qualitative descriptions.

Part III

Myogenic

The fibre itself misbehaves. One loud entity, three silent ones.

Myotonia

loud on EMG8:03

Delayed relaxation after a voluntary contraction or after percussion.

Mechanism
  • Na⁺ or Cl⁻ channel dysfunction.
  • The membrane depolarises too easily, or repolarises too poorly.
  • Result: repetitive action potentials after the stimulus has ended.

Lower the chloride conductance

gCl 100%

One stimulus at the start. As Cl⁻ conductance falls, the fibre keeps firing by itself. The threshold in this model is illustrative, not a measured value. Cl⁻ carries most of the resting conductance of muscle (about 70–80%)bg, so losing it removes the brake on depolarisation. During activity K⁺ accumulates in the T-tubules; without the Cl⁻ shunt this depolarises the fibre back to thresholdbg.

Bedside
  • Grip: make a fist or shake hands; the hand opens slowly.
  • Percussion: tap the thenar eminence; the thumb stays adducted.
  • 14-step test: repeated stair climbing reveals leg myotonia.
  • Tongue myotonia and lid lag: look up for a few seconds, then quickly down; the lid trails. Typical of Na⁺-channel myotonia.
  • Warm-up: myotonia eases with repetition. Named in the talk's summary.
  • Paradoxical myotonia: worsens with repetition and with cold. Points to SCN4A paramyotoniabg.
EMG
  • Myotonic runs: sustained rhythmic discharges of single muscle fibres.
  • Rate and amplitude wax and wane: a dive bomber, or a motorbike revving.
  • Runs are often incomplete: only waxing, or only waning.
  • Short exercise and cooling tests (Fournier patterns) help separate CLCN1 from SCN4A myotoniabg.

Real or mimic? Listen to both

11:43
Myotonic run · waxes and wanes
Pseudomyotonic (HFD) · abrupt, flat

High-frequency discharges start and stop suddenly with constant rate and amplitude. Seen in Pompe disease, other glycogen storage diseases and necrotising myopathies. They do not mean a myotonic syndrome. Sounds are synthesised from each pattern's rules, not recorded from patients.

Causes
  • Primary channelopathy (non-dystrophic myotonia): mutations in the Na⁺ (SCN4A) or Cl⁻ (CLCN1) channel genes.
  • Secondary channelopathy (myotonic dystrophy DM1, DM2): RNA missplicing of targets such as CLCN1 produces dysfunctional channel isoforms.

Sort the features: dystrophic or non-dystrophic?

Test
  • Non-dystrophic: multigene panel.
  • Dystrophic: Southern blot or a repeat-specific test. DM1 and DM2 are repeat expansions, which a normal panel misses.
Treat
  • The myotonia itself is treatable.
  • First choices: mexiletine or lamotrigine. Both are effective, with different side-effect profiles.
  • Mexiletine is the only approved anti-myotonic drug (in the EU, for non-dystrophic myotoniabg); others are off-label.
  • Newer drugs are reserved for refractory cases.

Rippling muscle disease

silent19:11

Waves rolling under the skin after a stretch or a light tap. A caveolinopathy. In bodybuilding videos it is called "alien gains".

Provoke the three signs

Stretch shows rippling. Tap shows a rapid contraction, then a mound.
Signs
  • Rippling (harp phenomenon): waves after stretch or slight percussion.
  • PIRC, percussion-induced rapid contraction: hard to tell from percussion myotonia by eye. The EMG settles it.
  • PIMM, percussion-induced muscle mounding (myoedema): a focal tonic lump. Also seen in hypothyroidism.
Mechanism
  • Caveolae are flask-shaped membrane pits built from caveolin-3 (the muscle isoform) and cavins 1–4.
  • A membrane reservoir that opens on stretch: the visible wave.
  • They organise T-tubules and modulate RyR, and so intracellular Ca²⁺.
  • They help hold the resting potential through K⁺ channels.
  • They bind the dystrophin–glycoprotein complex and help cluster ACh receptors at the NMJ.
Causes
  • CAV3: RMD, but also LGMD (formerly 1C), isolated hyperCKaemia, a pseudometabolic picture (exercise intolerance, myalgia, rhabdomyolysis), distal myopathy, isolated cardiac disease.
  • CAVIN1: secondary caveolin-3 deficiency; rippling with lipodystrophy and glucose intolerance.
  • Acquired: linked to myasthenia or thymoma; can respond to immunotherapy.
Test
  • CAV3, then CAVIN1.
  • If no mutation is found: cavin-4 IgG (Milone group).

Brody disease

silent27:30

Stiffness after sudden, fast exercise, because calcium is not pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Cytosolic Ca²⁺ after one burst

Mechanism
  • SERCA1 (gene ATP2A1) deficiency, mainly in type 2 fibres.
  • Cytosolic Ca²⁺ stays high, so the fibre stays contracted.
  • The gene was found partly by studying "pseudomyotonia" in Romagnola cattle.
Clinical
  • Very rare, autosomal recessive, starts in childhood.
  • Stiffness on phasic exercise, with myalgia, contractures and mild weakness.
  • Some cases of malignant hyperthermia under general anaesthesia.
EMG
  • No discharges: a silent contracture.
Test
  • ATP2A1 sequencing.
  • For a variant of uncertain significance: show SERCA activity and expression in muscle (immunohistochemistry, immunoblot).
Relatives
  • Calcium-related myopathies: myalgia, cramps and contractures are possible, but weakness usually dominates.

Part IV

Neurogenic

The motor nerve fires by itself. 32:42

  • Activity can start at the soma of the lower motor neuron, along the axon, in its distal terminals, or at presynaptic voltage-gated K⁺ channels.
  • The four classic signs: cramps, fasciculations, myokymia, and the muscle spasms of tetany.
  • Terminology: "spasm" strictly means involuntary contraction of central origin, as in spasticity or multiple sclerosis.
  • Each of these can be benign or idiopathic, or a sign of neuromuscular disease.

Muscle cramps

dense interference34:33

Sudden, very painful, supramaximal contraction. Transient, and relieved by stretching. Reported by about 37% of the general population, more with age.

Inside a cramp: motor-unit recruitment

each row = one motor unit

Units are recruited progressively and fire faster, then drop out slowly. Fasciculations or high-frequency discharges can appear just before or after.

Clinical
  • Sudden onset, very painful.
  • Supramaximal contraction, transient.
  • Stretching relieves it.
  • Post-cramp pain is common, probably local hypoxic and metabolic injury.
Origin
  • Still debated.
  • Most experiments point to the distal motor nerve terminals.
  • The spinal cord modulates it: activating the antagonist helps, and the H-reflex changes after a cramp.
Physiological
  • An expected response to extreme conditions: intense exercise, severe electrolyte imbalance.
  • Mostly distal muscles.
  • Benign, not progressive.
Idiopathic
  • More frequent than physiological cramps.
  • Nocturnal cramps; benign cramp-fasciculation syndrome.
  • Benign, not progressive.
Symptomatic
  • A warning sign of disease.
  • Motor neuron disease, neuropathy, plexopathy, radiculopathy, myopathy.
Non-neuro causes
  • Electrolyte disturbances
  • Medications
  • Pregnancy
  • Endocrine disease
  • Liver or kidney dysfunction
Work-up
HistoryFrequency, muscles involved, new medications, consistent triggers or relievers.
ExaminationRed flags of a neuromuscular disease.
LabsExclude non-neurological causes. Rarely revealing.
EMG / NCSWhen neuromuscular disease is suspected; it guides what comes next.
Treatment, as ranked in the talk 41:16
No drugs
  • Hydration; avoid triggers; stop suspect medications.
  • Regular stretching protocols. A Cochrane review suggests less severity, not fewer cramps.
Best evidence
  • Vitamin B complex, diltiazem.
  • Trial each for at least two weeks.
  • The 2010 AAN review also lists naftidrofuryl (Level C)bg.
Not first line
  • Quinine: effective, but risks arrhythmia, allergic reactions and haemolytic-uraemic syndrome.
Some patients
  • Magnesium (evidence doubtful).
  • Pickle juice or other acetic-acid drinks.
  • Gabapentin, pregabalin.
ALS
  • Mexiletine reduced cramps in a clinical trial.

The evidence base is thin. The last evidence-based review of cramp treatment dates from 2010.

Fasciculations

popcorn44:42

Brief twitches that recur sporadically and wander across the muscle belly.

EMG
  • Spontaneous, random, irregular discharges of single motor units.
  • Variable waveform from one discharge to the next.
  • Muscle ultrasound detects them well. The talk recommends Nens van Alfen's EAN lecture.

Listen: irregular pops

Origin
  • Debated.
  • In early ALS: probably the soma of the lower motor neuron.
  • As denervation progresses: more distal, with nodal hyperexcitability in the axon and terminals.
Benign
  • After exercise, mostly calves and lower limbs.
  • Cramp-fasciculation syndrome.
  • Anxiety.
Symptomatic
  • Motor neuron disease: ALS (tongue), Kennedy disease (perioral, on speaking).
  • PNH, multifocal motor neuropathy, plexopathy, radiculopathy.
  • In ALS, fasciculation potentials alongside chronic neurogenic change count as evidence of ongoing denervation (Awaji criteria)bg.

Myokymia

marching soldiers49:48

Continuous, undulating movement under the skin, "like worms". A fasciculation that never stops.

One unit, regular bursts

EMG
  • Bursts of 3–6 spikes from a single motor unit, at a uniform rate.
  • The bursts repeat regularly and sound like marching soldiers.
Focal
  • Eyelid myokymia, e.g. after sleep deprivation.
  • Facial muscles.
  • Post-radiation, e.g. one side of the tongue.
  • Facial myokymia also suggests MS, a brainstem lesion or Guillain–Barrébg.
  • Limb myokymia in a previously irradiated plexus favours radiation plexopathy over tumour infiltrationbg.
Generalised
  • Think peripheral nerve hyperexcitability (next).
Case from the talk
  • Young patient, five years of continuous twitching.
  • Hyperhidrosis and diarrhoea.
  • Worm-like movements in the calves, thighs and small foot muscles; calf hypertrophy.
  • EMG: myokymic discharges and fasciculations; no neuromyotonic discharges or triplets.
  • CASPR2 weakly positive. Very good response to immunotherapy (steroids).

Peripheral nerve hyperexcitability

spectrum52:46

Three syndromes on one clinical spectrum. The further along, the more systems involved and the likelier antibodies are.

Move along the spectrum

    Likelihood of VGKC-complex antibodies (qualitative)
    Antibodies
    • Against the VGKC complex: CASPR2 more often than LGI1.
    • Can be paraneoplastic or occur with other autoimmune disease.
    • Ask for CASPR2 and LGI1 specifically: VGKC-complex positivity without either has little clinical meaningbg.
    EMG
    • Myokymic discharges and fasciculations.
    • Neuromyotonic discharges: rarely captured. They can resemble myotonic discharges, but fire much faster (about 150–250 Hzbg) with a "pinging" sound.

    Neuromyotonic discharge · "pinging"

    Treat
    • Acquired PNH is treatable.
    • Mild: symptomatic drugs.
    • Immunosuppression: steroids.
    • Refractory: rituximab.
    Hereditary
    • Episodic ataxia type 1: episodic ataxia plus myokymia, especially facial. Gene: KCNA1 (Kv1.1)bg.
    • Neonatal-onset epilepsy with myokymia: linked to a centrally expressed K⁺ channel, KCNQ2bg.
    • HINT1 neuropathy: early, severe axonal neuropathy with neuromyotonic discharges.
    • Schwartz–Jampel (perlecan, HSPG2): generalised hypertrophy, stiffness, blepharophimosis, bone deformities. Both myotonic and neuromyotonic discharges. Perlecan clusters acetylcholinesterase, so its loss may leave more ACh in the cleft; it may also affect Na⁺/Cl⁻ channels. 56:07

    Tetany

    doublets57:53

    Hyperexcitability of nerve, both peripheral and central, caused by low plasma calcium. More common than the rare syndromes above.

    Mechanism
    • Low Ca²⁺ increases Na⁺-channel permeability.
    • The nerve membrane depolarises more easily.
    • Hypomagnesaemia and alkalosis (e.g. hyperventilation) produce the same picturebg.
    Bedside
    • Trousseau: ischaemia from a cuff produces the "obstetric hand".
    • Chvostek: tapping the facial nerve makes the face twitch. Low specificity: also positive in some healthy peoplebg.
    EMG
    • Doublets, triplets, multiplets, e.g. recorded during a Trousseau manoeuvre.
    • Not specific: also seen in PNH and other states of nerve hyperexcitabilitybg.
